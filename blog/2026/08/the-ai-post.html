Why I don't use generative AI

I will admit that this blog post is (mostly) the result of me being asked multiple times, in different contexts, why I don't use genAI. So, to be frank, the reason I refuse to use genAI is because I believe it is epistemologically incompatible with research, and I refuse to engage with technology that (for me) amounts to methodological heresy. GenAI corrupts the way we as researchers educe meaning, which influences public trust in science.

Below, I dive deeper into some of my misgrievances. I anticipate that this blog post will be a living document as more and more data comes out, so feel free to check in every-so-often if this is of interest.

Part 1: Fundamental Misgrievings

This section is to outline fundamental issues with genAI that I have yet to see any (reasonable) counterargument for.

The environmental cost

Data centers kill communities, environments, and local resources.

The water used to cool data centers leaks back into the community's water table. In some communities, water with nitrates — a known carcinogen — is forced into the local aquifers quicker than it would have otherwise because of data centers. Data centers are often built in drought-prone regions, putting further strain on local communities by creating heat islands — even when companies try to mitigate these issues, the local community does not see any direct benefit. This results in people not being able to drink their own water or they risk being exposed to toxic chemical concentrate.

Data centers also pump methane, one of the largest contributors to climate change, into surrounding communities. The noise they generate causes noise pollution, which has been shown to cause higher blood pressure and anxiety in humans. Some data centers release particulates into the air, which also raises the risks of cancer.

Even before the rise of data centers, we were already on target for major environmental collapse for many ecosystems. For animals sensitive to aquatic or atmospheric conditions, data centers put further strain on already-strained systems, freshwater ones. Birds and other fauna that rely on low light/sound to survive are significantly affected by the noise and light pollution generated by data centers.

Tech companies do not want you to think about these problems. They do not care about the communities they are hurting, and have made a consolidated push to silence this rhetoric. This follows from a long history of other companies doing the same, from tobacco to lawn darts; they also rely on you, the consumer, to push this narrative. Don't fall for it.

The human cost

There's a lot I can say here, and I've had to actively cut down most of my writing on this subject to avoid burying the lead.

First and foremost, genAI weights do not come from the ether. They are trained by the blood of underpaid workers around the world, but mostly in the global South. These workers are not unionized, do not get health benefits, suffer backbreaking hours, and have high rates of suicide/PTSD. These are your neighbors, friends, and family.

Synthetic content necessitates ingesting intellectual property without reparations. Right now, companies are destroying physical media to feed their models. Perhaps this is a signpost for the future of information access, where all content is mediated through bullshit generators. We'll see.

Like I said, there are many, many, many other human harms I could cover here. GenAI is being used to deny insurance claims. They induce psychosis and have been directly responsible for the suicides of multiple people. They perpetuate the biases of its training data! For a highly detailed list of citations, I highly recommend this Google Doc.

Part 2: Why you (as a researcher) should not be using genAI

The only thing genAI outputs is statistically significant strings of characters, pixels, etc. It has no concept of an outside world. This is why they're called bullshit generators — it has no concept of truth, correctness, fairness, or safety, no matter what level of "prompt engineering" or "safeguarding" you do. It cannot give you an answer to "why" it generates what it did.

Outside of its ethical concerns, this is, by and large, the main reason why I never use these things in my research. In order to articulate problems — generate research questions, design methodologies, interpret results —, researchers need to be aware of how epistemological framings (i.e., what constitutes "truth") matter in a given field, and articulating how research questions apply to those framings. This creates a metaphorical chain of custody for ideas, and helps cognize the limitations of certain framings.

By using genAI to generate any part of the resource process, EVEN partially (the so-called "human-in-the-loop"/human-AI collaboration pipeline, whatever that means), it throws that chain of responsibility out the window. Synthetic data cannot explain when an approach is more efficient, what critiques to anticipate, what research questions are better, or when content is/isn't "clear", because it doesn't have a theory of mind to grasp these ideas in the first place. They are less than helpful, especially considering how generated output is, by design, biased!

Even for seemingly benign use-cases, such as cataloging a big dataset, there is still no way for a researcher to articulate why genAI classified data the way it did. You can speculate why, sure, but I have yet to see any evidence that this is no better than guesswork — if I run that model again, on the same dataset, using the same prompt, there's a chance that it will generate an entirely different catalog. It's entirely non-reproducable, and thus useless to future scientists (and audiences). What's more, there are already established methods on how to do something like this efficiently (NLP, ML, etc.) that don't rely on guesswork.

In part, this is also why I don't advocate for LLMs to generate user-facing coding projects or prototypes wholesale. No matter how well a LLM generates code, if you don't understand what the code is doing, you're creating a blackbox. AI-generated code is insecure and can lead to real-world harm. See Dr. Frank Elavsky's fantastic posts (here and here) about this: the point is, when we design things, we are thinking and responsible for those things. Every design decision, whether that be about the data structure we use, what stack, or whether to use a dynamic or static language, are all decisions we weigh the pros and con(sequences) for.

Consequently, your brain literally atrophies when you use genAI. By outsourcing your work to LLMs, the way you think about problems becomes greatly homogenized. Dr. Hank Lee et al.'s great paper describes how users with less confidence in their work have higher confidence (and reliance) on genAI output. This sentiment is consistent with other findings in various domains, even with career professionals. People who consider themselves AI natives/literate overestimate their performance when using AI. We are a frog being boiled alive by automation bias.

Arguably, genAI negates the point of a PhD in the first place. To borrow from one of my favorite PhD-related blogs ever (shoutout to Yoshi!), being able to peer inwardly to understand what interests you, why, and how that relates to your discipline, is part of scientific inquiry. Your research may contribute to an existing knowledge base, but it might be the case that existing methodologies are not suitable for your research. For example, your work might act as a bridge to connect two disparate fields that have never intersected before, or be a complete ontological departure from an established field entirely. For interdisciplinary fields like my own, the latter two points are particularly salient. If I used genAI to generate methodologies for me, even partially, there would be no way for me to recognize, let alone build, novel solutions to problems because of genAI's reliance on historic data and normalized output.

Perhaps the real point I'm really trying to hammer home is this: research is, fundamentally, a deeply personal process of philosophical becoming which, by technological design, genAI can't (and never will be able to) compensate for. You are cheating yourself by relegating this process to exploitation machines that only seek to statistically normalize your ideas into homogeneity.

Closing thoughts

For this next part, I deeply apologize dear reader, but I have to guilt trip you a teeny tiny bit: consider what your degree means. Outside of the pure research context, your level of education signals a level of authoritative knowledge on a subject, no matter what Sam Altman says. The United States, and much of the world, is in a post-truth era, where scientific authority and established knowledge is called into question in favor of pseudoscience. At the very least, you have a duty to explain what you put into the world.

To wrap things up, remember that everything you do during your PhD has a purpose. Creating a powerpoint from scratch has a purpose. Designing a prototype has a purpose. Designing methodologies, no matter the tedium, has a purpose. Learning how to read, engage, and critique papers has a purpose! For the majority of the work you do as a PhD student, you do not need to use genAI and, in fact, it will only hurt you, and society, in the long run. Do not take part in the Torment Nexus.

Post-Script

This section is to address some anticipated critiques to this post.

"GenAI is already here, so it's better that we adapt."

I point to the history of tobacco, thalidomide, slavery, segregation, and a many number of other atrocities. Appeals to tradition have never stood against scrutiny, and they shouldn't be the bar for why we adopt something. Just because we are already doing something doesn't mean we have to continue to do it. We can be wrong, and we can change course.

"Like a hammer, genAI is a tool. It just matters how you use it."

Every tool we make is a coming together of people, places, and things. There are politics associated with every tool we use, decisions that were/were not made, and which people were in the room and which were not. Just as a bomb is a tool, the politic of how genAI is produced, (mis)used, and its resulting harms fundamentally necessitates critique.
